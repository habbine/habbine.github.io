Burden of Proof: Medical Liability

Habbine Estelle KimMay 20, 2025

In the landmark Mercier judgment, the Cour de cassation (French Supreme Court) recognised that the relationship between a doctor and their patient is contractual in nature, giving rise to a duty of care on the part of the practitioner, which is characterised as an obligation of means (obligation de moyens):

“whereas a genuine contract is formed between the doctor and the patient, comprising, on the practitioner’s part, the undertaking (…) to cure the patient, (…) or at least to provide care that is not of just any kind (…) but conscientious, attentive and, save in exceptional circumstances, consistent with established scientific knowledge; whereas the breach, even involuntary, of this contractual obligation gives rise to liability of the same nature, which is likewise contractual (…)”

(Cass. civ., 20 May 1936, Dr Nicolas v Mercier)

Since the adoption of Law No. 2002-303 of 4 March 2002, known as the Kouchner Law, supplemented by the Law of 30 December 2012, medical liability law has undergone a significant shift aimed at making it easier for victims of a medical accident or error to obtain compensation. Among other things, this legislative framework gave access to the Regional Conciliation and Compensation Commission (Commission Régionale de Conciliation et d’Indemnisation, CRCI) and to the National Office for Compensation of Medical Accidents (Office National d’Indemnisation des Accidents Médicaux, ONIAM), allowing broader compensation, in some cases even in the absence of fault. Since that reform, the liability of healthcare professionals has been governed by an autonomous principle of tort-based liability, in accordance with Article L. 1142-1, I, paragraph 1 of the Public Health Code, under which healthcare professionals may be held liable only where there is fault in the performance of the medical act, except where the harm results from a defect in a health product.

This regime nonetheless remained subject to a classic evidentiary requirement: the burden of proving fault lay with the patient (the claimant).

It is precisely on this point that the Cour de cassation reversed its case law in 2024, establishing a rebuttable presumption of fault where there is a failure in the traceability of care.

(Cass. Civ. 1, 16 October 2024, No. 22-23.433)

The case arose from a dispute over post-operative complications following the breakage of a metal guide wire during a hip arthroscopy. Because of persistent pain, the patient subsequently underwent an arthroplasty. Relying on a court-appointed expert’s report and on the recommendations of the French Arthroscopy Society (Société Française d’Arthroscopie, SFA), the lower courts found that, in line with good practice, the procedure should have begun with an injection of air followed by an injection of saline solution into the joint. However, this preparatory step was not mentioned in the operative report, even though the practitioner stated that he performed it systematically.

By a judgment of 29 September 2022, the First Chamber, Section 6, of the Aix-en-Provence Court of Appeal held that the harm suffered by the patient could have resulted either from an individual anatomical particularity or from the practitioner’s failure to follow the above recommendations. However, in the absence of definite proof of such a failure, the court concluded that no fault could be attributed to the surgeon and therefore ruled out his liability:

“the after-effects suffered by Mr [E], directly linked to the breakage of the wire, could have two distinct origins: either his anatomical constitution, given that he also suffered from osteoarthritis, or a failure by the surgeon to follow the SFA recommendation, which was merely an unproven hypothesis, so that the patient had not established the existence of fault on the part of the surgeon.”

The First Civil Chamber of the Cour de cassation, on the basis of Article L. 1142-1, I, paragraph 1 of the Public Health Code and Article 1353 of the Civil Code, overturned this analysis. It reiterated that a doctor must provide appropriate care, meaning care consistent with established scientific knowledge. The Court held that a breach of this obligation may be presumed, in particular where the operative report is incomplete, preventing the patient from verifying that the care provided actually met the required scientific standards.

“It follows from these provisions that healthcare professionals are liable for the harmful consequences of preventive, diagnostic or treatment acts in the event of fault, and that the burden of proving both fault and a causal link with the alleged harm lies with the claimant. However, where information on the patient’s care is missing or insufficient, making it impossible for the patient or their successors to ascertain whether the preventive, diagnostic or treatment acts performed were appropriate, it is then for the healthcare professional to prove that they were. (…) In so ruling, whereas, in the absence of evidence establishing that the aforementioned recommendation had been followed, it was for the doctor to prove that the care had been appropriate, the Court of Appeal infringed the above provisions.”

With this decision, the Court reverses the burden of proof: it is now for the healthcare professional to show that the care provided was consistent with established scientific knowledge where the medical records do not make it possible to establish this. Such a solution amounts to presuming the practitioner’s fault where there is a failure in the traceability of care.

This development marks a major shift in the assessment of medical liability and raises several questions. In particular, it calls into question the very nature of the fault: does it lie in the failure to follow scientific recommendations, or in the deficiency in the traceability of care?

The implications of this case law are considerable. By establishing a presumption of fault where the operative report is insufficient, the Cour de cassation requires healthcare professionals to exercise greater vigilance in drafting medical records and ensuring the traceability of procedures. Compliance with professional recommendations and protocols must now be rigorously documented so that, in the event of a dispute, the practitioner can rebut the presumption of fault and prove that the care was in fact consistent with scientific requirements.

In this respect, Article R. 4127-8 of the Public Health Code provides that every doctor must provide care as follows:

  • “Within the limits set by law and taking into account established scientific knowledge, the doctor is free to prescribe what he or she considers most appropriate in the circumstances.
  • Without neglecting the duty of moral support, the doctor must limit prescriptions and acts to what is necessary for the quality, safety and effectiveness of care.
  • The doctor must take into account the advantages, drawbacks and consequences of the various possible investigations and treatments.”

While this reversal of case law undeniably strengthens patients’ rights (in particular the right to freedom of choice, to information and to receive quality care), it imposes on healthcare professionals a heightened obligation of traceability and transparency of medical information.

In this context, it becomes imperative for healthcare professionals to apply greater rigour both in obtaining informed consent and in drafting operative reports, and more generally in documenting clinical practice in writing.
