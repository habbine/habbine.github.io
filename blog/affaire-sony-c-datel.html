Intellectual property

Sony v Datel

Habbine Estelle KimOct 29, 2024

Intellectual property · Software · Games · Computer programs · EU law · Copyright · Gameplay

In Sony v Datel, the Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU) was asked to rule on the scope of protection afforded to computer programs under Directive 2009/24/EC. The dispute set Sony, the rights holder for PlayStation game consoles, against Datel, a company that develops and markets software and accessory devices allowing modifications to Sony games, such as adding “cheats” or removing gameplay restrictions.

(CJEU, 17 Oct. 2024, Sony v Datel, Case C-159/23)

Facts

Sony holds an exclusive licence to market PlayStation game consoles and associated games on the European market. Until 2014, it marketed, among other products, the PlayStation Portable (PSP) and PSP games, including the game “MotorStorm: Arctic Edge” (the Game).

Datel develops, produces and distributes software, including add-on products for Sony game consoles that offer cheat tools for video games. These include the “Action Replay PSP” software and the “Tilt FX” device which, together with software of the same name, allows the PSP console to be controlled by movements in space. These programs are compatible exclusively with original Sony games.

Action Replay PSP is run by connecting the PSP console to a computer and inserting a USB stick into the console, through which the software is loaded. Once the console has been restarted, the user can access an additional tab entitled “Action Replay” within the interface, which offers gameplay options not provided by Sony at that stage. For the Game in question, these options include removing all restrictions on the use of the “turbo” (booster) mode and unlocking not only certain drivers but also others that would normally become available only after a certain number of points had been reached.

As for Tilt FX, a sensor connected to the PSP console allows it to be controlled by movements in space. It also requires a USB stick to be inserted into the console. Doing so adds a tab to the interface which removes certain restrictions and allows unlimited use of the turbo in the Game.

Datel’s software is installed by the user on the PSP console and runs simultaneously with the Game software. It neither modifies nor reproduces the object code, the source code, or the internal structure and organisation of the Sony software used on the PSP console. It merely temporarily alters the content of variables that Sony’s games place in the PSP console’s RAM and use while the Game is running, so that the Game runs on the basis of those altered variables.

Procedure

Sony argued that Datel’s software infringes its exclusive right to “alter” its programs (within the meaning of Article 4(1)(b) of Directive 2009/24/EC) by modifying variables in RAM, even without interfering with the source code or object code of the protected games.

On that basis, Sony sought an order that the marketing of these devices and programs cease, together with compensation for the loss it claimed to have suffered.

The Regional Court of Hamburg, Germany (Landgericht Hamburg) partly upheld Sony’s claims in its judgment of 24 January 2012. That judgment was reversed on appeal by the Higher Regional Court of Hamburg (Oberlandesgericht Hamburg), which dismissed Sony’s action in its entirety. The referring German court then heard an appeal on a point of law (Revision) against the judgment of the Oberlandesgericht Hamburg. It considered it necessary to determine whether the use of Datel’s software infringed the exclusive right to alter a computer program, as provided for in Paragraph 69c(2) of the UrhG, held by Sony, which required an interpretation of Article 1(b) of Directive 2009/24. In those circumstances, the Federal Court of Justice of Germany (Bundesgerichtshof) decided to stay its proceedings pending the CJEU’s answer to the questions referred for a preliminary ruling, which was delivered by judgment of 17 October 2024:

  1. Whether the modification of variables in RAM by third-party software constitutes an infringement of the right to alter a protected program.

A program running simultaneously with the protected program could alter the content of the “variables” that the protected program has stored in the computer’s RAM and uses while it runs.

When interpreting a provision of EU law, account must be taken of its wording, its context, the objectives of the rules of which it forms part and, where appropriate, its legislative history (CJEU, 23.11.2023, C-260/22, para. 22).

The Court therefore examined Article 1 of Directive 2009/24:

The protection granted to “computer programs” under Article 1(1) of Directive 2009/24 extends to “the expression in any form of a computer program”, with the exception of the ideas and principles underlying any element of the program.

Article 1(3) of Directive 2009/24 provides that a computer program is protected if it is “original”, i.e. if it is the author’s own intellectual creation. No other criteria apply to determine its eligibility for protection, and protection also covers preparatory design work leading to the development of a program, provided that it is such as to result in a program at a later stage. Protection is thus limited to the intellectual creation as reflected in the text of the source code and the object code and, consequently, to the literal expression of the computer program in those codes, which respectively constitute a set of instructions according to which the computer is to perform the tasks laid down by the program’s author.

The “forms of expression” of a computer program are those which permit it to be reproduced in different computer languages, such as the source code and the object code (CJEU, 22.12.2010, C-393/09, para. 35).

The following do not constitute a “form of expression” of a computer program within the meaning of Directive 2009/24:

· the graphic user interface of a computer program, which does not enable the program to be reproduced but merely constitutes one element of the program by means of which users make use of its features (CJEU, 22.12.2010, C-393/09, paras 41 and 42);

· the functionality of a computer program, or the programming language and the format of data files used in a computer program in order to exploit certain of its functions (CJEU, 02.05.2012, C-406/10, paras 39 and 40);

· the elements by means of which users make use of the functionality of a computer program, without enabling that program to be reproduced or subsequently created;

· ideas, procedures, methods of operation or mathematical concepts as such (CJEU, 13.11.2018, C-310/17, EU:C:2018:899, para. 39), such as the ideas and principles underlying the various elements of a program, including those underlying its logic, algorithms and programming languages.

The Court held that the content of the variables used by a protected program is an element of that program by means of which users make use of its features, and does not fall within the protection conferred by Article 1(1) to (3) of Directive 2009/24, since it does not enable that program to be reproduced or subsequently created:

“Article 1(1) to (3) of Directive 2009/24/EC of the European Parliament and of the Council of 23 April 2009 on the legal protection of computer programs must be interpreted as meaning that the content of the variable data transferred by a protected computer program to the RAM of a computer and used by that program in its running does not fall within the protection conferred by that directive, in so far as that content does not enable such a program to be reproduced or subsequently created.”
  1. Whether the concept of “alteration” within the meaning of Article 4(1)(b) of Directive 2009/24 covers modifications made by third-party software running simultaneously, without directly altering the source or object code of the protected program.

Article 4(1)(b) of Directive 2009/24 provides for:

“the translation, adaptation, arrangement and any other alteration of a computer program and the reproduction of the results thereof, without prejudice to the rights of the person who alters the program”.

This question arose because, as regards the rights holder’s exclusive rights, it is the unauthorised reproduction, translation, adaptation or transformation “of the form of the code in which a copy of a computer program has been made available” that infringes the author’s exclusive rights. In this respect, it should be borne in mind that the legal regime for the protection of computer programs does not confer a monopoly capable of hindering independent creation and technical progress. A computer program is designed to communicate and interact with other components of a computer system and with users. To that end, it requires a logical and, where appropriate, physical interconnection and interaction allowing all software and hardware components to function fully in coordination with other software, hardware and users.

On this point, the Court held that:

  • “In the light of the answer given to the first question, there is no need to answer the second question.”

The Court found that the content of the variables placed by the program in RAM while it runs falls outside the scope of protection. It was therefore unnecessary to answer the second question. Consequently, Sony cannot seek a ban on these cheat programs.
