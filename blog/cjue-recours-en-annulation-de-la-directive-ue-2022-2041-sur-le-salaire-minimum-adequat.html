CJEU: Action for Annulment of Directive (EU) 2022/2041 on Adequate Minimum Wages

Habbine Estelle KimJul 31, 2025

The European Commission presented its proposal for Directive (EU) 2022/2041 in October 2020, following on from President Ursula von der Leyen’s political commitment to introduce a “fair minimum wage” in the European Union. It also expressed its intention to “promote collective bargaining on wages in all Member States”. The Directive seeks to establish a harmonised framework while respecting Member States’ sovereignty in setting adequate minimum wages, in response to wage inequality and the need to ensure a decent standard of living for workers. The Directive calls for minimum wages to be updated regularly, for transparent criteria to be adopted for setting them, and for mandatory consultation of the social partners. It also recommends the use of indicative reference values, such as 60% of the gross median wage or 50% of the gross average wage.

The Directive distinguishes two key dimensions of minimum wage adequacy:

  • The relative dimension, which compares the minimum wage with the wages of the working population, and which tends to strengthen as minimum wages converge towards the reference values.
  • The absolute dimension, which assesses whether the minimum wage ensures a decent standard of living, taking into account the cost of living, household composition, additional income and tax and social security systems.

Action for annulment of the Directive before the CJEU

Under the principle of conferral enshrined in Article 5(2) TEU, the European Union may act only within the areas conferred on it by the Treaties.

Denmark, supported by Sweden, brought an action for annulment under Article 263 TFEU asking the Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU) to annul Directive (EU) 2022/2041 in its entirety, on the ground that the European Parliament and the Council had exceeded their competences (Case C‑19/23). According to the applicants, Articles 153(1)(b) and 153(2)(b) TFEU allow the European Parliament and the Council to lay down minimum requirements in the field of working conditions. However, Article 153(5) TFEU expressly excludes EU competence with regard to pay.

In this context, Advocate General Nicholas EMILIOU concluded on 14 January 2025 that:

  • The Directive exceeds the competences conferred on the Union, in particular by regulating, even indirectly, the field of pay. From this perspective, Directive (EU) 2022/2041 would be incompatible with Article 153(5) TFEU, which expressly excludes any harmonisation in the field of pay. It would thus disregard the principle of conferral laid down in Article 5(2) TEU, under which the Union acts only within the limits of the competences conferred on it by the Member States.
  • Article 4(1)(d) and Article 4(2) of Directive 2022/2041 may be regarded as severable from the rest of the text. Their annulment would affect neither the coherence nor the applicability of the other articles, which could continue to produce their effects. Accordingly, and in the alternative, should the Court consider partial annulment sufficient, it is suggested that it uphold the Kingdom of Denmark’s alternative claim and annul only the above provisions, without calling the Directive as a whole into question.
  • The EU legislature could not validly have based Directive (EU) 2022/2041 on Article 175 TFEU. That provision is primarily intended to strengthen the economic, social and territorial cohesion of the Union, in particular through the allocation of Union funds. Directive 2022/2041 could not have been adopted on that basis merely because it sought to reduce disparities in socio‑economic levels across the Union, to promote upward convergence and to foster a more harmonious development of the Union. Moreover, according to settled CJEU case law, where a Treaty provision expressly excludes harmonisation in a given area, the legislature may not rely on another legal basis so as to circumvent that exclusion. Article 153(5) TFEU (lex specialis) expressly excludes any harmonisation in the field of pay. By relying on Article 175 TFEU, a more general provision, to regulate wages indirectly, the legislature would have breached this rule of interpretation and unduly extended the scope of the Union’s competence.

What do the statistics show?

According to Eurostat data, as at 1 July 2025, EU Member States fall into three distinct groups based on the amount of their gross monthly national minimum wage:

  • Group above €1,500/month: Luxembourg, Ireland, the Netherlands, Germany, Belgium and France, with minimum wages ranging from €1,802 (France) to €2,704 (Luxembourg).
  • Intermediate group from €1,000 to €1,500/month: Spain, Slovenia, Poland, Lithuania, Greece, Portugal and Cyprus. Wages range from €1,000 in Cyprus to €1,381 in Spain.
  • Group below €1,000: Croatia, Malta, Estonia, Czechia, Slovakia, Romania, Latvia, Hungary and Bulgaria, with levels ranging from €551 in Bulgaria to €970 in Croatia.

Between July 2015 and 2025, average annual minimum wage growth was particularly strong in several Central and Eastern European countries, notably:

  • Romania (+13.0%),
  • Lithuania (+12.3%),
  • Bulgaria (+11.0%),
  • Poland (+10.2%).
  • Conversely, countries such as France (+2.1%) and Malta (+2.9%) saw more moderate growth.

Frequently asked questions on the minimum wage in the European Union

Q1: Which EU countries have no national minimum wage?

Denmark, Italy, Austria, Finland and Sweden.

Q2: What is the range of minimum wages in Europe?

As at 1 July 2025, minimum wages range from €551 in Bulgaria to €2,704 in Luxembourg.

Q3: How does the EU Directive influence the setting of minimum wages?

It requires transparent criteria, regular updates and consultation of the social partners, while proposing indicative reference values to guide Member States.

Q4: What are the challenges in ensuring adequate minimum wages?

Guaranteeing a decent standard of living requires taking into account not only the minimum wage but also the cost of living, household composition and tax and social security systems.
