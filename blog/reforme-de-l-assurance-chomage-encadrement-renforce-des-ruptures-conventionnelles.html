Unemployment Insurance Reform: Tighter Rules on Conventional Terminations

Habbine Estelle KimJul 30, 2025

Conventional termination (rupture conventionnelle) was introduced into the French Labour Code (Code du travail), at Articles L1237-11 to L1237-16, by Law No. 2008-596 of 25 June 2008 on the modernisation of the labour market:

"The employer and the employee may jointly agree on the terms of termination of the employment contract between them. Conventional termination, which is distinct from dismissal and resignation, cannot be imposed by either party. It results from an agreement signed by the parties to the contract. It is subject to the provisions of this section, which are intended to guarantee the parties' freedom of consent."

Conventional termination:

  • is based on mutual agreement between the employer and the employee. It allows a permanent employment contract (contrat à durée indéterminée, CDI) to be ended by common consent of the employer and the employee.
  • must be free, informed and voluntary. Neither party may be subjected to pressure, threats or violence when the agreement is signed.
  • may be requested by the employer

may be requested by any employee, including:

  • protected employees (subject to authorisation by the labour inspector)
  • employees on leave for a work-related accident or illness
  • employees on maternity leave.

Exclusions:

  • Employees on fixed-term contracts (CDD), who may use early termination instead
  • Apprentices
  • Employees on a CDI who are still within their probationary period
  • Civil servants and public-sector staff (both tenured and contractual).
  • In addition, the employee may challenge the arrangement before the Employment Tribunal (Conseil de Prud’hommes), in particular on grounds of defective consent, within 12 months of the date of approval.

Before this mechanism was introduced, an employee had only two options:

  • Resign, thereby giving up any entitlement to unemployment benefits
  • Be dismissed.

Procedure

Conventional termination is governed by a strict procedure:

  • One or more preliminary meetings between the employee and the employer, to discuss freely the terms on which the permanent employment contract (CDI) will end.
  • Signature of a termination agreement setting out the terms of the separation and the amount of the specific conventional termination indemnity, which may not be lower than the statutory or collectively agreed severance indemnity.
  • A withdrawal period of 15 calendar days, during which either party may go back on its decision;
  • An application for approval (homologation) sent to the administration
  • An administrative approval period of 15 working days from receipt of the file by the DREETS (formerly DIRECCTE).

During the approval period, the employee continues to work as normal. The employment contract remains fully in force until the agreed end date stated in the termination agreement.

⚠️ The contract end date cannot be changed or postponed if the employment contract is suspended (paid leave, sick leave, family-event leave, etc.).

If no response is given within the prescribed period, the agreement is deemed approved.

If the administration refuses approval, the decision must state the reasons for the refusal (for example, failure to comply with a step of the procedure, or failure to meet the minimum amount of the conventional termination indemnity).

Once the termination has been approved, the employee may receive unemployment benefits paid by France Travail (formerly Pôle emploi), provided the eligibility criteria are met, in particular having worked as an employee for at least 6 months (i.e. 130 days or 910 hours worked) in the last 24 months (the last 36 months for employees aged 55 or over).

Stricter conditions under consideration by the French government

As part of the unemployment insurance reform, the government, led by François Bayrou and with Minister of Labour Astrid Panosyan-Bouvet, is preparing an overhaul of the conventional termination mechanism.

The objectives: to correct abuses, reduce public spending and ensure that this form of amicable separation is used within a tighter framework.

Conventional termination, now widely used in the private sector, is increasingly criticised for opportunistic misuse and its growing cost to the public finances.

The government is considering a set of structural measures intended to curb the mechanism's excessive attractiveness and to make employers more accountable, notably from a Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) perspective. The main avenues of reform are as follows:

  • Extending the withdrawal period, currently 15 days, to reinforce the considered nature of consent.
  • Stronger administrative oversight, particularly in very small businesses and SMEs (TPE/PME) and in sectors with high termination rates.
  • More rigorous prior review to prevent fraudulent or abusive terminations.
  • Preventing mass terminations in certain companies in financial difficulty.
  • Extending the waiting period before entitlement to unemployment benefits begins (currently between 7 days and 5 months depending on the indemnities paid), to limit so-called "convenience" departures.
  • Capping indemnities
  • Revising the amount and duration of benefits, with a possible reduction in entitlements.
  • Increasing the employer contribution
  • Restricting access for certain profiles: employees on sick leave, older workers, cases of repeated terminations.
  • A new minimum qualifying period: 8 months of work in the last 20 months (compared with 6 months in 24 at present), leading to a reduction of the maximum benefit period to 15 months.
  • In parallel, the administrative authorities are now exercising greater vigilance when approving conventional terminations.
  • Approval may be refused where the termination is intended to avoid an economic dismissal or to circumvent a job protection plan (plan de sauvegarde de l’emploi, PSE).
  • Widespread use in companies in difficulty may trigger an investigation.

Reasons for the proposed reform

The government intends to reform unemployment insurance as part of its policy of rationalising public spending, with a view to generating:

  • Savings of between €2 billion and €2.5 billion a year over the 2026–2029 period;
  • Up to €3 billion to €4 billion in annual savings by 2030.

In this context, it is worth noting the upward trend in conventional terminations, confirmed by the latest available statistics:

Number of conventional terminations:

  • 2019: 439,464
  • 2020: 428,296
  • 2021: 467,503
  • 2022: 503,465
  • 2023: 514,854
  • 2024: 514,627

(an increase of 17.1%, from 439,464 to 514,627 conventional terminations)

Number of resignations (for comparison):

  • 2019: 1,748,113
  • 2020: 1,407,010
  • 2021: 1,801,824
  • 2022: 2,147,635
  • 2023: 2,172,099
  • 2024: 2,041,573

(an increase of 16.78%, from 1,748,113 to 2,041,573 resignations)

(all company sizes and all sectors) according to data published by the Dares, a ministerial statistical service forming part of the official statistical system (service statistique public, SSP).

Indeed, the conventional termination mechanism is now criticised for being misused, in particular when it serves to circumvent a dismissal (sometimes disguised or pre-arranged) or to mask a resignation while still opening entitlement to unemployment benefits. These practices, more frequent in times of economic strain, raise growing concerns about the fairness of the system and help undermine the financial sustainability of unemployment insurance.

Against this background, the ongoing unemployment insurance reform aims to regulate the use of conventional termination more closely, by providing for new rules and stronger controls. The objective is clear: to prevent the mechanism from being diverted for the purposes of financial bargaining or as a source of interim income.

Abolition or reform?

The Minister for Labour and Employment, Astrid Panosyan-Bouvet, stressed on BFMTV/RMC that conventional terminations account for nearly 25% of unemployment benefit payments, a figure considered worrying in the current budgetary context.

"We want benefits to become a springboard back into work again, not a lasting source of funding for convenience terminations." — Astrid Panosyan-Bouvet, Minister for Labour and Employment

She also pointed to the level of benefits, considered higher than for other types of contract termination, as well as a longer benefit period:

  • 15 months for a conventional termination,
  • compared with 14 months for an economic dismissal,
  • and 13 months for a voluntary resignation (according to Unédic data).
  • "Extending the waiting period is one of the solutions that could be considered," the Minister said.

Interviewed on Franceinfo on 29 July, Patrick Martin, President of the Medef (the main French employers' federation), responded to the discussions on reforming conventional termination. While acknowledging the existence of "deviant behaviour" in the use of the mechanism, he called for moderation:

  • "Let's not throw the baby out with the bathwater. The mechanism needs to be corrected without being fundamentally called into question." — Patrick Martin, President of the Medef
  • He therefore argues for a targeted reform, without any structural challenge to the mechanism.

Several avenues of reform are therefore currently being discussed, notably extending the waiting period, i.e. the period before unemployment benefits actually start to be paid. This measure would discourage convenience departures and reduce the financial pressure on the unemployment insurance system.

He also pointed out that conventional termination has helped to calm the labour market by reducing tensions between employers and employees:

  • "We can see that the number of disputes before the Employment Tribunals has fallen sharply thanks to these conventional terminations." — Patrick Martin, President of the Medef

It seems appropriate to preserve the principle of conventional termination, which has done much to make the ending of employment contracts smoother while reducing litigation before the Employment Tribunals. However, in view of its misuse and growing budgetary impact, the government considers it essential to revise the way it is applied in order to correct these abuses. The aim of the reform is clear: to maintain contractual flexibility for businesses while ensuring the sustainability of the unemployment insurance scheme and preserving the principles of national solidarity.
