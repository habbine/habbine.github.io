Deploying AI in the Workplace and the Prerogatives of the CSE

Habbine Estelle KimMay 21, 2025

The summary order issued by the Nanterre Judicial Court (tribunal judiciaire) on 14 February 2025 underlines the importance of respecting the prerogatives of the social and economic committee (comité social et économique, CSE), including during the experimental phases of deploying new technologies, in particular those based on artificial intelligence.

(Nanterre Judicial Court, summary order (ordonnance de référé), 14 February 2025, RG No. 24/01457)

The facts

On 25 January 2024, the management of a company presented a project to deploy five applications incorporating artificial intelligence (AI) technologies, including Finovox, Synthesia, Notify, Semji and MetIQ.

The CSE found that some of the applications covered by the project had already been in a pilot phase for several months, involving actual use by employees and the training of dedicated teams. In the committee’s view, this pilot phase, which entailed the use, even partial, of these new tools by all the employees concerned, could not be treated as a mere trial preceding the presentation of a finalised project. On the contrary, it had to be characterised as a first implementation of the software applications concerned, which fall within the CSE’s information and consultation procedure. Yet, at the time of this finding, the committee had neither been consulted nor delivered an opinion on these tools.

In these circumstances, by writ dated 17 June 2024, the CSE summoned the company before the summary proceedings judge (juge des référés), seeking, first, an order requiring the immediate opening of the consultation procedure provided for by the French Labour Code (Code du travail) and, second, the suspension of the implementation of the disputed tools until that procedure had been completed. The committee argued that the company had implemented the tools without waiting for the outcome of the consultation, in breach of its statutory prerogatives. In its view, this amounted to a manifestly unlawful disturbance (trouble manifestement illicite) as well as an obstruction (entrave) of its prerogatives.

The decision

By summary order of 14 February 2025, the Judicial Court held that the pilot phase could not be treated as a mere trial or test phase. It considered that such use amounted to a first implementation of the applications concerned. It was therefore liable to trigger the obligation to consult the CSE beforehand, pursuant to Article L. 2312-8 of the Labour Code. That provision requires the CSE to be consulted prior to any introduction of new technologies likely to alter employees’ working conditions.

Recommendations for businesses

  • Anticipation: build CSE consultation into the very first phases of any technology project.
  • Transparency: provide the CSE with complete, accurate and accessible information on the tools under consideration.
  • Dialogue: establish a regular and constructive exchange with the CSE to analyse the impact of the technologies on working conditions.
